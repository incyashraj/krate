# The road to 3D, including heavy games

From what exists today to a runtime that can carry a real 3D game, in stages
that each ship something usable rather than one long build with a demo at the
end.

Every number below was measured on this machine, not estimated.

## Where we actually are

Software 3D is **already working** as of today: `gfx.scene3d` binds to a
widget, takes a camera, a light and triangles, depth-tests them, and publishes
the frame through the same image path that puts photos on screen. No GPU
dependency, all three operating systems, because nothing new touches the
adapters.

Measured, spinning cube, this laptop:

| Resolution | Frames per second | Per frame |
|---|---|---|
| 320×240 | **352 fps** | 2.8 ms |
| 640×480 | **89 fps** (98 culled) | 11.3 ms |
| 800×600 | 60 fps | 16.8 ms |

Those are Stage 2 numbers, measured after threading. Before it: 207, 54 and 35
fps respectively.

That is a real answer, and it says two things at once. A stylised or low-poly
game at 640×480 is playable today. A modern 3D game is not, and no amount of
tuning the CPU rasterizer changes that — the gap is roughly a hundredfold, and
hundredfold gaps are hardware, not code.

## The four stages

### Stage 1 — Make software 3D genuinely usable — **done**

- **Textures.** Uploaded once and referenced by handle, UVs per corner,
  interpolated in perspective rather than linearly. The linear version is the
  classic warped-floor artefact and is invisible on a wall facing the camera,
  which is why it survives until somebody builds a room.
- **A transform per draw call.** `place` takes a mesh plus translate, rotate
  and scale, so a thousand leaves are one mesh and a thousand cheap calls.
  Rotation happens around the model's origin before translation, because the
  other order orbits objects around the world origin.
- **Held-key state.** `events.key-held`, tracked host-side and cleared when a
  window loses focus — the only place that bug can be fixed, because the host
  is the only side that sees focus change.
- **Back-face culling.** Opt-in, 20% rather than the hoped-for half, and it
  turned out to fix a depth-fighting bug: front and back faces of a cube meet
  at exactly equal depth, and the later one was winning.

**Shipped:** `krate-cubes`, nine textured spinning cubes in 12 KB, in the
nightly replay on all three systems.

### Stage 2 — Make it fast enough to stop thinking about (1–2 weeks)

Still CPU. The wins available, in order of payoff:

- **Threads.** ~~The renderer is one core.~~ **Done.** Horizontal bands, one
  per core, no locks — each thread owns its rows. It gave 1.7×, not the 4–8×
  hoped for, and finding out why was the useful part: with fill parallel, the
  serial cost of converting the frame to RGBA became the bottleneck, costing
  more than the 3D itself (224 fps rendering against 69 with conversion).
  Fixing that took it to 89.
- **SIMD.** Four or eight pixels per instruction in the inner loop. Another
  2–4× on top.
- **Tile-based traversal.** Better cache behaviour than the current
  bounding-box walk once triangles get small.

**Ships:** 1080p at 60 fps for moderate scenes, entirely on the CPU. Worth
saying plainly: that is where most indie 3D games actually live.

### Stage 3 — The GPU path (3–5 weeks, and the honest one)

This is where "heavy 3D games" lives, and it is genuinely weeks.

- **`wgpu` behind the same interface.** The reason to design `scene3d` the way
  it is: an app that draws triangles today should not be rewritten when the
  triangles start going to a GPU.
- **Three backends, one behaviour.** Metal on macOS, Direct3D 12 on Windows,
  Vulkan on Linux. `wgpu` abstracts the API; it does not abstract driver bugs,
  and the parity work is the real cost.
- **Shaders inside the sandbox.** A shader is a program. The capability
  question — what can a guest's shader read, and can it be used to see another
  app's memory — has to be answered before this ships, not after.
- **A software fallback that never disappears.** Stage 1 and 2 become the path
  for machines with no working GPU, which is why they are worth building even
  though Stage 3 supersedes them on paper.

**Ships:** heavy 3D, and the answer to "why not just use a browser".

### Stage 4 — What a game actually needs beyond drawing (parallel)

None of this is graphics, and all of it blocks a real game:

- **Gamepad input.** Nobody plays a 3D game on a keyboard by choice.
- **Mixed audio.** ~~Playback exists; a game plays twenty sounds at once.~~
  **Done.** `load-sound` holds a decoded effect, `play-sound` starts a copy
  mixed with everything else. It was worth doing rather than leaving to apps:
  opening a stream costs about two milliseconds -- an eighth of a frame -- so a
  game firing a sound on the frame it happens would stutter. Mixing happens in
  the device callback with a fixed scratch buffer and try_lock, because that
  thread must never allocate or block.
- **Asset loading.** Meshes and textures from a bundle without hand-writing
  vertex arrays, which is what the samples do now.
- **Frame pacing.** Vsync and a stable frame clock, so motion is smooth rather
  than merely fast.

## The dependency question

Stage 3 means `wgpu`, and that is a real weight: it pulls in shader compilers
and platform graphics crates, and it can fail at runtime on a machine with a
broken driver — which every other Krate capability cannot do.

Three rules that follow, and they are the reason to write this down now:

1. **The GPU is a capability, not an assumption.** `gfx.gpu` already exists as
   a declared capability. An app asks; a person may refuse; the app is told,
   and can fall back to software rather than dying.
2. **A missing GPU is never fatal.** Stages 1 and 2 remain the fallback
   forever. This is also why they ship first.
3. **`wgpu` is not in the tree until Stage 3 starts.** Adding a heavy
   dependency "so it is ready" costs every build, every release, and every
   platform container — and the arm64 Linux release this week failed on
   exactly that kind of transitive weight.

## What could make this wrong

Written down so it can be checked rather than defended:

- ~~**Threads may disappoint.**~~ **They did, and the plan was right to say
  so.** 1.7× rather than 4–8×, because the frame handoff is serial and became
  the bound the moment fill stopped being it. Amdahl, exactly as advertised.
  The remaining serial work is one memcpy of the finished frame.
- **`wgpu` in a sandbox may be harder than expected.** Handing a guest a GPU
  is the first capability that touches a driver directly. If the security
  answer is unsatisfying, Stage 3 waits.
- **The interface may not survive contact with a real game.** `scene3d` was
  designed against a spinning cube. A real one will want instancing,
  transparency ordering and skinned meshes, and some of that will change the
  shape. Better found by building a game than by imagining one.

## Sequencing

Stages 1, 2 and 4 need nothing that does not already exist and are worth doing
in that order. Stage 3 is a separate decision with a real cost, and it should
be taken when a game exists that Stage 2 cannot carry — not before.
